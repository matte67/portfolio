## A low price is only part of the decision

A cheaper package may contain less. A promotion may require a loyalty card or multiple items. Saving on groceries may mean another stop. Comparing isolated prices leaves much of the decision unexplained.

Spesino starts with the list: what you need, how much, and with which preferences. Offers become possible ways to fulfil that need.

## From search to a grocery list

The journey connects product search, supermarket selection, travel, and preferences. Users can look for a broad category or a specific product. The list keeps requests and quantities explicit before searching for a purchase combination.

## Making comparisons understandable

I designed the model to distinguish requested quantities from actual purchases: whole packages, surplus quantities, formats, and promotional conditions. A comparison should explain the proposal and expose any requests it cannot cover.

When travel and time enter the calculation, they are explicit preferences. Estimates depend on available data and search limits; a partial result must remain recognisable as partial.

## Changing data, clear boundaries

The project separates domain rules, contracts, use cases, adapters, and presentation. Offer ingestion, the catalogue, and alternative calculations have distinct responsibilities. Offers have validity dates; an unavailable source requires a dedicated state and a way to retry.

Next.js and React handle presentation, while application services coordinate rules and data access. The list’s meaning remains independent of the data format returned by an individual retailer.

## Status and direction

Spesino has a public interface and continues to evolve as a prototype. Images show development demonstrations; catalogue availability and results depend on active sources. No unmeasured average savings or usage outcomes are claimed.
