## Better grocery shopping starts before the supermarket

A package looks cheaper, but contains less. A promotion requires two items. Another supermarket has a better price, but adds a detour. Preparing a grocery shop means bringing these details together, often from different leaflets, notes, and websites.

I designed Spesino to support the complete decision: discover offers, turn them into a list, choose where to buy, and take a usable plan into the store.

## Start with offers or with your own list

**“Let’s see what is worth buying this week.”** The catalogue supports browsing products and promotions, searching by name or brand, and narrowing the selection with filters. Each card brings price, format, validity, conditions, and store availability together. An interesting product can go straight into the list.

**“I already know what I need.”** People arriving with a list can write it directly. Both starting points lead into the same journey: selected offers stay connected to their products while missing items are added.

## From “I need pasta” to a purchase quantity

A broad category and an exact package express different intentions. Search preserves that distinction: someone can ask for pasta without choosing a brand, or specify a particular product and confirm its quantity.

For example, two 500 g packages and one 1 kg package fulfil the same need, but can have different prices and conditions. The model separates the request from the purchase: the person defines the need, and comparison considers ways to fulfil it.

## Price and its conditions belong together

A package price becomes useful when its quantity and unit price are known. Discounts work the same way: loyalty cards, minimum purchases, and formats belong to the decision. Cards make those conditions available before a product is added.

Comparison also lets people examine alternative offers for the same request. This is an important part of the experience: understand a proposal and change it while preserving the connection to what was needed.

## Is another store worth the journey?

A lower combination of product prices can require more stops. Spesino compares product cost and, when data allows, the trade-off with travel. Store selection, travel mode, and the value assigned to time make the person’s preferences explicit.

Results distinguish covered products from requests still needing attention. A single-supermarket alternative has a practical purpose too: deciding whether the benefit of a second stop justifies the journey.

## A plan to use between the shelves

After selection, the plan becomes a checklist organised by supermarket. Quantities, conditions, and the order of stops remain available. Purchases can be checked off and filtered to show what is left.

The list also keeps requests that offers did not cover, so they remain part of the shop. Actual spending can be recorded separately from the estimate: a different checkout price does not retrospectively rewrite the original comparison.

## Return to the products you buy often

For milk, pasta, or other regular purchases, repeating the same search adds work. Followed searches retain the product or category and the stores to check. People can return to relevant offers, pause a search, and resume it when needed.

This extends the product beyond a single list. Work on alerts separates choosing what to follow from activating a device; push delivery needs its own deployment verification.

## When it begins with a recipe

With [Boccone](/en/work/boccone), the need can start before the list: choose a dish, adjust servings, and mark ingredients already available. Missing ingredients become a list; people who want offer and route comparisons can continue with Spesino.

Both belong to Nodale, with a common account entry point and distinct responsibilities. Boccone preserves the cooking and community context; Spesino develops the purchase decision. Selected list components are also shared so familiar actions carry across the transition.

## Build around changing data

I worked on user experience, architecture, and full-stack development. Quantity, package, and promotion rules are separate from presentation; use cases coordinate search and comparison; adapters handle catalogues, sources, and external services.

A promotion has a validity period and a store context. The catalogue preserves that context, and the interface distinguishes a search without matches from an unavailable source. These choices also make incomplete results understandable.

Spesino continues to evolve as a public prototype. Screens illustrate the product through development examples; catalogue availability, routes, and personal services depend on deployment and active sources. Actual savings still need to be measured against real purchases.
